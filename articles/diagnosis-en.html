Why HR Problems Are Often Diagnosed Wrong — and How to Find the Root Cause Before You Waste Time and Budget

A practical framework for turning an HR complaint into a testable hypothesis, a root-cause diagnosis, and a measurable intervention.

The Problem Is Not Always What You See

Low performance, high turnover, slow hiring and low engagement are outcomes we can observe, not necessarily causes. A common HR mistake is to start with a familiar solution: training for poor performance, more pay for resignations, or another hiring campaign for vacancies. A stronger approach starts with a different question: what is actually happening, to whom, when, and under what conditions? Precise problem definition lowers solution cost and increases the probability of impact.

1. Define the Problem as a Measurable Gap

Write the current state, the expected state, and the gap. Instead of "performance is weak," write: "average target attainment fell from 91% to 74% over three months in one customer-service team while headcount stayed stable." This identifies the metric, time period, population and magnitude of the issue.

2. Separate Symptoms from Causes

Ask first: what do we see? Then: what could explain it? Absence may reflect workload, inflexible scheduling, transport, health or a management practice. Turn explanations into hypotheses rather than conclusions. Multiple plausible hypotheses are safer than one attractive story.

3. Segment Before You Average

A company average can hide the real pattern. Segment by manager, role, location, shift, tenure, contract type, and other legitimate variables. If turnover is 18% overall but 39% in one team during the first six months, "the labor market" is unlikely to be the whole explanation. Segmentation turns a metric into a pattern.

4. Build and Test Hypotheses

Use 5 Whys or a fishbone diagram to generate hypotheses, not to manufacture proof. Then collect evidence through structured interviews, attendance and performance data, exit reasons, surveys, workflow times and manager observations. Ask what evidence would confirm or reject each hypothesis.

5. Interview Employees for Diagnosis

A diagnostic interview asks more than "Are you satisfied?" Ask when the problem started, where it happens most, what happens immediately before it, what makes the task difficult, what has already been tried, and what would change if the barrier disappeared. Look for repeated patterns rather than treating one interview as universal truth.

6. Test Four Families of Causes

Many people problems can be explored through four families: capability and skill, motivation and expectations, systems and process, and environment and management. A skill gap needs enablement; unclear expectations need clarity; a broken approval process needs redesign; weak management behavior needs management intervention. Not every performance problem is a training problem.

7. Match the Intervention to the Cause

If role ambiguity is the cause, clarify expectations and measures. If a skill gap is real, design job-linked learning with practice and follow-up. If approvals create a bottleneck, redesign the workflow. If managers are not giving feedback, change management behavior. Every intervention should answer: which cause are we targeting?

8. Measure Impact Before Declaring Success

Set a baseline, a primary outcome metric and guardrail metrics. When speeding up hiring, do not celebrate a lower time-to-fill while ignoring quality of hire, probation success, candidate experience and cost. Compare before and after; where possible, use a comparison group or unaffected units.

Recurring Diagnostic Mistakes

Common mistakes include treating a manager complaint as fact, using averages without segmentation, confusing correlation with causation, starting with a ready-made training course, treating an engagement survey as a complete diagnosis, ignoring job design, and choosing a solution before defining success. AI can summarize patterns and generate hypotheses, but human judgment remains accountable for diagnosis.

A Practical Diagnostic Meeting

Use five minutes to define the outcome, ten to review evidence, ten to identify patterns, ten to generate hypotheses, ten to specify missing evidence, and ten to choose a small intervention with an owner and review date. Leave with one page: problem, scope, baseline, three hypotheses, evidence needed, intervention, owner, timing and success measures.

Conclusion

Strong HR is not the function with the largest menu of solutions. It is the function that knows which solution fits which cause. Better diagnosis reduces wasted initiatives, improves the quality of management conversations and moves HR from complaint handling toward evidence-based decision intelligence.

A 12-Question Diagnostic Test

Before approving an initiative, ask: what outcome deteriorated; since when; for whom; by how much; what is the baseline; what are the three strongest hypotheses; what evidence supports each; what evidence is missing; what can the team control; what is the lowest-cost testable intervention; who owns it; and when will results be reviewed? If these answers are missing, you are probably still defining the problem.

How to Write a Strong HR Hypothesis

A useful hypothesis links a possible cause to an outcome in a testable way. Example: "Early-tenure exits may be related to job expectations that were unrealistic at the point of hiring." Then define the evidence: exit reasons, gaps between the advertised role and actual work, onboarding feedback and differences by hiring source. If evidence does not support the hypothesis, change the hypothesis rather than defending it.

Move from Diagnosis to a Small Experiment

Not every HR problem requires a large transformation project. Test an intervention in one team or process, define a baseline, a measurement window, a success metric and guardrails. Run it, learn, then scale. This reduces the risk of spending heavily before the diagnosis is credible.

Pre-Launch Diagnostic Checklist

Did you quantify the problem? Separate symptom from cause? Segment the data? Listen to stakeholders? Test multiple explanations? Link the intervention to the cause? Assign an owner? Set a baseline? Define guardrails? And does the team know when to stop the initiative if evidence shows little impact?

Practical takeaway: Do not start with the solution. Define the problem, collect evidence, test hypotheses, then choose an intervention whose impact can be measured.
