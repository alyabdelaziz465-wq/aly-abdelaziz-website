Hiring Is Not a CV-Writing Contest

A CV is a marketing document, not a complete test of job capability. When hiring relies on keyword similarity or first impressions, bias can rise and predictive value can fall. Evidence-based hiring redesigns the process around one question: what evidence shows that this candidate can succeed in this job?

1. Start with Job Analysis, Not the Advertisement

Define critical outcomes, tasks, behaviors, knowledge and skills. Separate genuine requirements from historical preferences that do not predict performance. This reduces inflated requirements and expands the realistic talent pool.

2. Turn Competencies into Evidence

If problem solving matters, do not only ask whether the candidate is a problem solver. Request a specific example, a work sample or a realistic case. Every critical competency should have observable evidence and a scoring rule.

3. Structure the Interview

Use a consistent question set for candidates applying to the same role, clear anchors for strong answers, trained interviewers and independent scoring before group discussion. Structure reduces the effect of chemistry, charisma and snap judgments.

4. Use Work Samples Where Practical

A customer-call simulation, simple data analysis, professional email or prioritization exercise can reveal capability that a CV cannot. Keep the exercise job-relevant, reasonably short, clearly instructed and scored against explicit criteria.

5. Build a Scorecard

Weight critical competencies, for example communication 25%, problem solving 25%, functional knowledge 30% and collaboration 20%. The score is not an automatic decision; it creates a common language and makes disagreements visible.

6. Protect Fairness and Candidate Experience

Check whether particular groups drop out at a specific stage and whether assessments are genuinely job-related. Explain the process, respect candidate time and provide updates. Candidate experience is part of the employer brand.

7. Use AI as an Assistant, Not a Judge

AI can summarize CVs, extract skills, draft interview questions, schedule interviews and compare evidence against a clear rubric. It should not become an opaque gatekeeper that decides who is "best" without explainability, human review, data controls and bias checks.

8. Measure Quality of Hire

Closing vacancies is not the full outcome. Track performance at 3, 6 and 12 months, probation success, retention, time to productivity, manager and employee experience, and hiring cost. The objective is better decisions, not simply faster movement.

Recurring Hiring Mistakes

Common errors include inflated job descriptions, CV-only screening, unstructured interviews, different questions for different candidates, intuition-only decisions, weak documentation, ignoring source quality and failing to learn from past hires.

A 30-Day Implementation Plan

Week one: analyze roles and competencies. Week two: build questions, rubrics and work samples. Week three: train interviewers and run a controlled pilot. Week four: review evidence, candidate experience and early outcomes. Start with one critical role and scale what works.

Conclusion

A strong hiring system does not search for the perfect candidate on paper. It collects multiple job-relevant signals and uses them consistently and fairly. The closer selection gets to real work, the more defensible, measurable and learnable hiring decisions become.

Applied Example: Customer Service Hiring

For a customer-service role, do not rely on "good communication" in an interview. Define evidence: a difficult-customer simulation, a professional written response, prioritization of three complaints and a short policy-comprehension test. Score each on a 1–5 scale with behavioral anchors, then compare the assessment with post-hire performance. The selection system can learn from its own outcomes.

How to Audit Hiring Quality

Track each stage: applicants, pass-through rate, stage time, source, offer acceptance, withdrawal and later quality-of-hire. If many candidates fail one assessment, do not automatically assume candidates are weak. Review the assessment difficulty, instructions, job relevance and candidate experience.

When AI Helps—and When It Becomes Risky

AI is useful for reviewable support work: summarization, extraction, question drafting, scheduling and structured comparison. It becomes riskier when historical or linguistic signals become an opaque judgment of suitability. Practical rule: the higher the impact on a person’s opportunity, the stronger the need for explainable human review.

A Fast Implementation Chain

Job → critical outcomes → competencies → evidence → question or work sample → rubric → interviewer training → documented decision → quality-of-hire review. If any link is missing, the process can easily drift back toward personal intuition.

Practical takeaway: Do not start with the solution. Define the problem, collect evidence, test hypotheses, then choose an intervention whose impact can be measured.
