HR STRATEGYWhy HR problems are often misdiagnosed — and how to build a better diagnosisDiagnosis → evidence → intervention → follow-up

Many HR interventions fail before implementation begins because the organization starts by choosing a solution rather than defining the problem. A low-performance case may be treated as a training issue when the real cause is unclear expectations, weak management, poor work design, missing resources or conflicting priorities.

1. Start with the problem, not the solution

Define the observable outcome: what is happening, where, since when, for whom, and what business or people impact is visible. Separate facts from interpretations. “Performance declined after the team changed managers” is evidence to investigate; “the employee is not motivated” is a hypothesis.

2. Build a cause map

Test five dimensions: expectations, capability, resources/process, management, and context. Ask what changed, what has remained stable, and whether the pattern is individual or systemic.

3. Choose the smallest effective intervention

If expectations are unclear, clarify them. If capability is missing, develop it through practice. If the process blocks performance, redesign it. If management behavior is the issue, coach and hold accountable. Use discipline only when the evidence shows a conduct or sustained performance issue that requires it.

4. Implementation mechanism

For each intervention define an owner, action, timeframe, evidence of completion and review point. Use a short cycle: diagnose, act, observe, adjust. Avoid launching a large HR program before testing the smallest viable change.

5. Where AI can help

AI can organize case information, surface patterns, generate diagnostic questions, summarize documented evidence and propose alternative hypotheses. It should not decide who is “the problem,” infer sensitive traits, or replace human judgment in consequential employment decisions.

Practical takeaway

Good HR is not the ability to name a policy quickly. It is the ability to identify the real cause, choose a proportionate intervention, execute it consistently and learn from the result.

TALENT ACQUISITIONFrom CV screening to evidence-based hiringRole clarity → evidence → structured assessment → decision

Recruitment becomes weaker when a job description is treated as a shopping list of keywords. Strong hiring begins with the outcomes the person must deliver, the capabilities required to deliver them, and the evidence that can demonstrate those capabilities.

1. Define the role through outcomes

Translate responsibilities into three to six critical outcomes. Then identify the knowledge, skills, behaviors and experience that are genuinely necessary. Separate “must have” requirements from preferences.

2. Design the assessment before reviewing candidates

For each critical capability, define what strong evidence looks like. Use structured questions, realistic work samples or job-relevant scenarios. Give candidates comparable opportunities to demonstrate the same capabilities.

3. Review CVs for evidence, not decoration

A strong CV makes contribution visible: what the candidate changed, improved, delivered, built or solved. Titles alone are weak evidence. A recruiter should be able to connect experience to the target role quickly without guessing.

4. Implementation mechanism

Create a simple hiring scorecard with evidence statements rather than vague impressions. Calibrate interviewers before interviews, document evidence immediately afterward, and conduct a short decision review that separates evidence from preference.

5. AI in recruitment

AI can help parse CVs, compare job requirements with candidate evidence, draft structured interview questions, summarize notes and identify missing evidence. Human reviewers should validate the output, monitor for bias and retain accountability for the decision. AI should not become an opaque gatekeeper.

Practical takeaway

The objective is not to find the candidate with the most keywords. It is to build a defensible chain from business need to role requirements to observable evidence to a fair decision.

PERFORMANCEWhen performance falls: a practical root-cause frameworkExpectations → capability → conditions → management → accountability

Performance problems are often handled emotionally because managers see the result before they understand the cause. A disciplined diagnosis reduces unfairness and makes improvement more likely.

The five-question sequence

What was expected? Confirm the standard and priority. Was it understood? Check whether the employee interpreted the expectation correctly. Could the person do it? Examine knowledge, skill and experience. Could the person realistically do it? Check workload, tools, process, dependencies and decision rights. What happened after feedback? Look for response, learning and sustained behavior.

Intervention ladder

Start with clarification. Then provide coaching and practice where capability is the gap. Remove process or resource barriers. Address management behavior when it contributes to the problem. If expectations are clear, support is reasonable and the gap persists, move to formal accountability according to policy.

Implementation mechanism

Document the gap in observable terms, agree on the expected behavior or outcome, define support, set a review date, and record evidence. Short, regular review cycles are usually more useful than waiting for an annual evaluation.

AI role

AI can help summarize feedback themes, identify recurring issues across documented cases and generate coaching questions. It should not label an employee as low potential or recommend disciplinary action without human review and context.

Practical takeaway

Fair performance management is a process of understanding, supporting, measuring and holding accountable — in that order.

CAREERHow to make a career shift into HR without starting from zeroTransferable strengths → evidence → positioning → experience

A career shift is not the erasure of previous experience. It is the translation of previous experience into value that the new field recognizes.

1. Choose a realistic entry point

Do not target “HR” as one generic destination. Identify a function where your existing strengths create an advantage: recruitment, learning, employee relations, HR operations, people analytics, performance or HR business partnering.

2. Translate transferable experience

Customer service can demonstrate stakeholder management. Sales can demonstrate relationship building and persuasion. Operations can demonstrate process management. Teaching can demonstrate learning and facilitation. The key is evidence, not relabeling.

3. Build proof

Create small projects: redesign a job description, build an interview guide, analyze an anonymized people dataset, design an onboarding journey, or write a performance improvement framework. Document the problem, your approach, output and learning.

4. Rebuild the CV and professional story

Lead with the target direction. Make transferable capabilities visible. Use achievement-oriented bullets only when supported by facts. Avoid pretending to have HR experience that you do not have.

5. AI as a career accelerator

AI can help map transferable skills, compare a target role with your current evidence, simulate interviews, improve drafts and create learning plans. The human must validate the story and ensure every claim is truthful.

Practical takeaway

The fastest credible career shift is usually built through targeted evidence and real practice, not through collecting courses alone.

AI & HRAI across the employee lifecycle: where to automate, where to augment, where to keep humans in controlAutomate → augment → human-led

The useful question is not “Where can we put AI?” It is “Where can AI improve the work without creating unacceptable risk?” A practical HR approach separates work into three zones.

Automate

Use automation for repetitive, rules-based activities with clear controls: document routing, reminders, standard communications, data preparation and workflow administration.

Augment

Use AI as decision support for research, summarization, drafting, pattern detection, skills mapping, interview-question generation and learning recommendations. The professional reviews the output and owns the decision.

Human-led

Keep high-impact decisions human-led: hiring outcomes, promotion, termination, disciplinary decisions and sensitive employee cases. AI may organize evidence, but context, fairness and accountability remain human responsibilities.

Implementation mechanism

For each use case define the objective, data sources, users, permissions, failure modes, human review point, escalation path and audit trail. Start with a contained pilot and compare quality, speed, risk and user experience before scaling.

Practical takeaway

Responsible AI in HR is not about using the most advanced model. It is about designing the right human–machine workflow.

FUTURE OF WORKFrom jobs to skills: how HR can prepare for a changing workforceWork → tasks → skills → capability → mobility

Traditional workforce planning starts with job titles. A more adaptive model starts with the work itself: which tasks create value, which capabilities are required, which skills are changing, and where people can move as the work changes.

1. Deconstruct work

Break priority roles into major activities and outcomes. Identify which activities are repetitive, which require judgment, and which are likely to be changed by technology or new operating models.

2. Build a skills view

Map current capabilities against future requirements. Focus on the few critical gaps that materially affect business priorities rather than creating enormous skill dictionaries nobody uses.

3. Create mobility pathways

Show how a person can move from current capability to adjacent roles through targeted learning, projects, mentoring and demonstrated experience.

4. Implementation mechanism

Choose a small set of strategic roles, create a capability map, identify gaps, launch practical development experiences and review movement every quarter. Link workforce planning, learning, internal mobility and recruitment instead of running them as separate programs.

5. AI and the future

AI will change tasks faster than many job titles change. HR therefore needs stronger capability in skills intelligence, work redesign, learning agility and responsible technology adoption.

Practical takeaway

The future-ready organization is not the one that predicts every future job. It is the one that can detect change, redeploy capability and learn faster.
